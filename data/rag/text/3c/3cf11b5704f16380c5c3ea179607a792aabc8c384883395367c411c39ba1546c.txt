Honorio   Delgado   430,   San   Martin   de   Pomes   15102,   Lima,   Peru ARTICLE   INFO Keywords: Aquaculture Freshwater   fishes Monogenoidea Peruvian   Amazonia ABSTRACT In   the   present   study   it   is   presented   a   list   of   species   of   Monogenoidea   with   their   fish-hosts   used   in   aquaculture   in the   Peruvian   Amazonia.   Fish   species   were   obtained   from   April   2018   to   April   2022   from   different   artificial   fish ponds   located   in   “Centro   de   Investigaciones   Fernando   Alcântara   Bocanegra"   (CIFAB)   of   the   "Instituto   de Investigaciones   de   la   Amazonia   Peruana   (IIAP)   in   Loreto-Peru   and   from   fish   ponds   of   the   "Centro   de   Inves¬ tigation   Carlos   Miguel   Castaneda   Ruiz”   from   IIAP,   San   Martin-   Peru.   A   total   list   of   fourty-eight   species   is   pre¬ senting   in   the   present   study:   one   species   from   Arapaima   gigas   (Schinz);   four   species   from   Colossoma   macropomum (Cuvier);   five   species   from   Myloplus   schomburgkii   (Jardine);   two   species   from   Piaractus   brachypomus   (Cuvier);   19 species   from   Pygocentrus   natterei   (Kner);   one   species   from   Prochilodus   nigricans   (Spix   &   Agassiz);   five   species   from Brycon   amazonicus   (Spix   &   Agassiz);   one   species   from   Chaetobranchus   flavescens;   three   species   from   Astronauts ocellatus   (Agassiz);   two   species   from   Cichla   monoculus   (Agassiz);   four   species   from   Pterigoplichthys   pardalis (Castelnau);   three   species   from   Calophysus   macropterus   (Lichtenstein);   and   three   species   from   Pseudoplatystoma punctifer   (Castelnau). 1   .   Introduction The   knowledge   of   the   biodiversity   of   Monogenoidea   parasites   in   fish¬ hosts   from   South   America   have   been   explored   from   different   authors and   registered   in   catalogues   and   checklists:   Nasir   and   Fuentes   (1983)   for Latin   America;   Kohn   and   Santos   (1989);   Thatcher   (1991);   Boeger   (2006) for   the   Neotropical   Region;   Kohn   and   Santos   (1989);   Kohn   and   Paiva (2000)   and   Cohen   et   al.   (2013)   for   South   American   countries;   Luque et   al.   (2016)   for   Peru.   (See   Figs.   1-13). Fish   species   commercialized   in   the   markets   of   Loreto,   Peru   come from   fishing   in   natural   environments   and   others   come   from   aquaculture production.   This   activity   has   been   experiencing   a   constant   growth   per year.   Within   the   main   species   cultivated   in   the   peruvian   Amazonia   and offered   in   the   markets   of   the   cities   stands   out   species   of   the   Arapaima- dae,   Serrasalmidae,   Bryconidae,   Cichlidae,   Loricaridae   and   Pimelodidae (García   Dávila   et   al.,   2018). The   aim   of   this   paper   is   to   update   the   list   of   species   and   hosts   of   South American   Monogenoidea   presenting   for   the   first   time   a   specific   checklist of   this   group   of   parasites   from   fish-hosts   used   in   aquaculture   in   the Peruvian   Amazonia. 2.   Material   and   methods Fish   host   species   were   obtained   from   April   2018   to   April   2022   from different   artificial   fish   ponds   located   in   “Centro   de   Investigaciones Fernando   Alcântara   Bocanegra”   (CIFAB)   of   the   "Instituto   de   Inves¬ tigaciones   de   la   Amazonia   Peruana   (IIAP)   in   Loreto-Peru   and   from   fish ponds   of   the   “Centro   de   Investigation   Carlos   Miguel   Castaneda   Ruiz” from   IIAP,   San   Martin-Peru. Species   collected   in   CIFAB-IIAP,   Loreto   Peru   were:   the   arapaima Arapaima   gigas,   the   black-band   myleus   Myloplus   schomburgkii,   the   black pacu   Colossoma   macropomum,   the   red-bellied   pacu   Piaractus   brachypo¬ mus,   the   red-bellied   piranha   Pygocentrus   nattereri,   the   black   prochilodus Prochilodus   nigricans,   the   dorado   Brycon   amazonicus,   the   giant   basket¬ mouth   cichlid   Chaetobranchus   flavescens,   the   oscar   Astronotus   ocellatus, the   peacock   bass   Cichla   monoculus   and   the   spotted   tiger   shovelnose catfish   Pseudoplatystoma   punctifer. Fish   were   kept   alive   until   their   parasitological   examination.   In   the laboratory,   gill   archers   were   removed   and   placed   in   vials   containing   hot water   (65   °C).   Each   vial   was   shaken   vigorously   and   96%   ethanol   was added.   The   content   of   each   vial   was   examined   using   a   dissecting   mi¬ croscope   and   helminths   were   removed   from   the   gills   or   sediment   using dissection   needles.   Some   specimens   were   mounted   on   slides   using Hoyer's   medium   to   determine   the   haptoral   structures   (bars,   anchors   and hooks)   and   male   copulatory   organ   (MCO)   and   the   accessory   piece. Others   specimens   were   stained   with   Gomori's   trichrome   (Humason 1979;   Boeger   and   Vianna   2006)   and   mounted   in   Canada   balsam   to determine   internal   organs.   Some   specimens   were   preserved   in   ethanol 96%for   future   DNA   analyses.   All   procedures   were   conducted   in   the “Laboratorio   de   Parasitologia   y   Sanidad   Acuicola"   of   the   “Instituto   de Investigaciones   de   la   Amazonia   Peruana"   (IIAP)   in   Iquitos,   Peru. (2010),   from   the   total   number   of   1064 valid   freshwater   fish   species   recorded   for   Peru,   approximately   800 belong   to   the   Amazonia.   Despite   this   richness,   few   parasitological studies   have   focused   on   the   fishes   inhabiting   their   hydrological   drain¬ ages.   Some   taxonomical   studies   and   reports   on   the   parasites   of   fishes from   the   Peruvian   Amazon   using   morphological   and   molecular   ap¬ proaches   on   monogenoids   have   been   conducted:   (Luque   et   al.,   2016) reported   from   freshwater   fishes   from   Peru   a   total   number   of   85   mono¬ genoids;   Mendoza-Franco   et   al.   (2016)   reported   six   other   new   species; Acosta   et   al.   (2019)   reported   five   new   species;   (Mendoza-Palmero   et   al. (2019)   with   one   species;   Morey   et   al.   (2019a)   with   four   new   species; Morey   et   al.   (2019b)   with   one   new   genus   and   one   new   species;   Morey et   al.   (2020)   with   one   species;   (Mendoza-Palmero   et   al.   (2020)   with   one new   species;   Morey   et   al.   (2021)   with   five   new   species;   Cayulla-Quispe et   al.   (2021)   with   one   new   species;   Morey   (2021)   with   three   new   species and   Feronato   et   al.   (2022)   with   one   new   species.   With   these   studies,   the number   of   species   of   Monogenoidea   reported   for   freshwater   fishes   from Peru   is   1 14.   The   list   presented   in   the   present   study   reported   50   species   of Monogenoidea   and   it   constitutes   the   first   checklist   of   monogenoids   from fish   species   used   in   aquaculture   in   the   Peruvian   Amazonia. For   A.   gigas   collected   from   the   Neotropical   region,   the   following monogenoids   have   been   reported:   Dawestrema   cycloancistrioides,   D. cycloancistrium   and   D.   punctatum   Cohen   et   al.   (2013).   In   Peru,   high   levels of   infestation   by   D.   cycloancistrium   affecting   specimens   of   A.   gigas   from fish-farming   have   been   reported   by   Luque   and   lannacone   (1991);   Del¬ gado   et   al.   (2013);   Mathews   et   al.   (2013);   Serrano-Martinez   et   al.   (2015) and   Murrieta   Morey   et   al.   (2020).   The   results   of   the   present   study   are similar   to   the   authors   mentioned   above,   reporting   only   to D.   cycloancistrium. For   C.   macropomum   the   monogenoids   Anacanthorus   spathulatus,   Lin- guadactyloides   brinkmanni,   Mymarothecium   boegeri,   Nozotothecium   euzeti, Notozothecium   janauachensis   have   been   reported   for   the   Neotropical   re¬ gion   (Cohen   et   al.,   2013). In   Peru A.   spatulathus,   A.   penilabiatus   and   M.   viatorum   have   been   cited   from fishes   collected   in   a   fish   pond   from   the   Peruvian   Amazon   (Gonzales et   al.,   2022).   In   the   present   study,   the   same   species   mentioned   above with   exception   of   A.   spatulathus   were   found   on   individuals   of P.   brachypomus. For   P.   nattereri   there   were   cited   the   following   species: A.   brachycirrum,   A.   calycinum,   A.   camelum,   A.   catalaoensis,   A.   falcatum, A.   junki,   A.   microphallum,   A.   anacanthorus,   A.   brazilensis,   A.   maltai,   A. neotropicalis,   A.   reginae,   A.   rondonensis,   A.   stachophallus,   A.   thatcheri, Calpidothecioides   orthus,   Calpidothecium   crescentis,   Calpidothecium   serra- salmus,   E.   aegidatum,   M.   galeolum,   N.   mizellei,   N.   minor,   N.   penetrarum, Pithanothecium   amazonensis,   Pithanothecium   piranhas   and   R.   piranhus (Cohen   et   al.,   2013).   In   the   present   study,   we   reported   for   the   first   time in   Peru   19   species   of   Monogenoidea   from   P.   nattereri   cultivated   in   an artificial   fish   pond. ForP.   nigricans   from   Peru,   the   following   monogenoids   were   reported: Rhinonastes   pseudocapsaloideum   from   the   gills   and   nasal   cavity   of P.   nigricans   collected   in   a   fish   farm   in   Loreto,   Peru   (Delgado   et   al.,   2013), Pina   et   al.   (2017)   reported   to   Apedunculata   discoidea,   T.   curimba   and T.   toksonum   from   P.   nigricans   collected   in   natural   environment   and   a   fish pond.   In   the   present   study   the   only   monogenoid   reported   was   T.   curimba. For   B.   amazonicus   the   monogenoids   A.   spiralocirrus,   Jainus   ama¬ zonensis,   Tereancistrum   kerri   and   Trinibaculum   braziliensis   were   reported for   fishes   from   Brazil   (Cohen   et   al.,   2013).   Morey   et   al.   (2021)   described five   new   species   of   Monogenoidea   from   the   gills   of   B.   amazonicus collected   in   natural   environment.   In   the   present   study,   all   species recorded   by   Morey   et   al.   (2021)   were   found   from   specimens   of B.   amazonicus   collected   in   a   fish   pond   in   the   Peruvian   Amazonia. The   first   study   about   the   parasitic   fauna   of   C.   flavescens   was   con¬ ducted   by   Tavares-Dias   et   al.   (2017),   who   found   the   monogenoids G.   spilocirra,   and   G.   elephus.   In   Brazil,   specimens   of   C.   semifasciatus   from Peru   were   parasitized   by   G.   tucunarense   (Mathews   et   al.,   2013).   In   the present   study,   we   reported   the   presence   of   Sciadicleithrum   edgari,   a parasite   described   for   the   first   time   from   the   gills   of   Satanoperca   jurupari collected   in   the   Araguari   River,   State   of   Amapá,   northern   Brazil (Tavares-Dias   et   al.,   2017).   In   the   present   study,   S.   edgari   is   cited   in   a new   host   and   this   is   the   first   report   in   the   Peruvian   Amazonia   of   a monogenoid   collected   from   the   gills   of   C.   flavescens.
